Field notes · Nº 03

Manifesto

The Friction
Is the Point

Writing software shouldn't write for you. On the difference between a tool that assists and one that supplants — and why the resistance matters more than the output.

M. Calloway June 01, 2026 12 min read

There is a gesture every writer learns to distrust: the second paragraph that arrives too easily. Not the one earned after four attempts and a walk around the block, but the one that simply materialises — fluent, competent, depressingly complete. This is the paragraph the new writing software is so eager to provide. It arrives before you knew what you wanted to say, which is precisely the problem.

We have spent the last several years watching the tools of composition absorb the work of composition. Autocomplete was the mild version: a word at the edge of your thought, offered up. What exists now is something different in kind. The software no longer finishes your sentences; it begins them. It proposes your argument, selects your tone, and, if you allow it, writes the whole thing while you watch — a stenographer for ideas you hadn't quite had yet.

The stenographer metaphor first appeared in a 1971 essay on Dictaphones and literary voice — it has never been more apt.

"The tools we use to write
are quietly deciding
who does the writing."

What assistance actually means

There is a real distinction between a tool that makes space for thought and one that fills it. A sharpened pencil is assistance. A spellchecker is assistance. Even a thesaurus, consulted in the right spirit, is assistance — it names a thing that was already forming in you, just out of reach. The new generation of writing tools are doing something else. They are not clarifying your intention; they are replacing it with a convincing approximation.

The approximation is the problem. Not the quality of it — you can usually tell — but what it does to the process. Every draft you accept from a language model is a draft you didn't write. Every sentence that comes pre-composed is a sentence you never had to inhabit. The inhabiting is the work. It is where the thing you're making becomes yours, becomes specific, becomes — against all odds — true.

The economy of effort

Defenders of AI-assisted writing often reach for the efficiency argument: we already use calculators for arithmetic; why not use language models for language? But the arithmetic analogy breaks down almost immediately. When you let a calculator multiply large numbers, you lose nothing except tedium. When you let a language model draft your paragraph, you lose the struggle — and the struggle is where most of the thinking happens.

This is not a romantic position about suffering. It is a practical one about how meaning actually gets made. The writer who cannot produce a sentence without assistance is, functionally, not producing sentences at all. They are curating them, which is a different activity, with different muscles, different outcomes, and a different relationship to the page.

"A writer only begins a book. A reader finishes it. The author's job is to be specific enough that the reader's imagination has something real to work against."

— John Updike, Paris Review, 1974

Updike wrote by hand until 1981, then switched to a Kaypro II running WordStar. He reportedly never used its search-and-replace function, preferring to re-read the entire document to locate a word.

The case for resistance

Folio is, admittedly, writing software. We are not arguing against writing software. We are arguing for a specific kind of relationship between the writer and the tool — one in which the tool withholds rather than provides. Not perversely, not as an ideological gesture, but because there are things a writer needs to do for themselves that the tool cannot do without doing harm.

Here is what we believe a writing tool should do: it should set type beautifully, because the look of the sentence affects how you feel about it while writing, which affects what you write. It should keep the draft safe and local, because nothing interrupts thought like the anxiety of loss. It should stay out of the way — not hide itself theatrically, but simply not speak unless spoken to. No suggestions. No sentence continuations. No analysis of your tone sent back to a server in another country.

Resistance, in this sense, is not cruelty. It is the same resistance a good editor provides: the one who says, "I think you know what this sentence is supposed to do — find a way to make it do that." The editor does not write the sentence for you. Neither should the software.

  1. 01
    Complete your sentences.

    The sentence not yet finished is the sentence still being thought. Completion forecloses the better version.

  2. 02
    Assess your tone.

    Tone is a consequence of having something to say in a particular moment. It cannot be optimised from outside.

  3. 03
    Suggest a structure.

    Structure is where argument lives. Outsourcing it outsources the thinking itself.

  4. 04
    Produce a draft on request.

    A draft produced for you is a draft that carries the fingerprints of no one in the room.

None of this is to say that every keystroke must be agonised over. Fluency is a real virtue, and some days the sentences come easily. But there is a difference between fluency that is earned — built up from reading, from revision, from the painful construction of earlier drafts — and fluency that is rented from a system trained on the writing of others. The first is the writer's. The second belongs to no one in particular, which is to say it belongs to everyone, which is to say it means very little.

The friction is the point. Not because difficulty is virtuous, but because writing without friction is not writing — it is transcription of a voice you have borrowed. The sentence you finish under resistance is yours in a way the generated one never can be. It carries the shape of your particular mind, the rhythm of your ear, the hesitation before the word you chose over the three words you rejected. That hesitation is the whole of it.
