Accessibility audits in an afternoon: a checklist for Palm Beach County nonprofits' websites

A four-hour accessibility review Suede AI uses on nonprofit websites in Palm Beach County, built so a staff member or volunteer can run it with free tools.

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede AI · November 15, 2026 · West Palm Beach, Florida

Picture a food bank volunteer in Lake Worth Beach trying to sign up for a Saturday shift using only a keyboard because of a wrist injury. The form looks fine, but the "Submit" button never takes focus, so the sign-up never happens and the nonprofit never learns why. Most accessibility failures on small nonprofit sites are this quiet, and most can be found in a single afternoon.

This checklist is the version Jason Colapietro and Suede AI use for quick reviews of nonprofit websites in Palm Beach County. It needs no paid software, a few free browser tools and about four hours.

Why nonprofits should run this audit first

A nonprofit's website does real work: it takes donations, recruits volunteers, publishes event dates and explains services to people who may be older, disabled, on an old phone or reading in a second language. Every barrier on the site falls on exactly the people the organization exists to serve.

There is also a practical reason. Pages that are accessible to assistive technology are easier for search engines and AI answer engines to read, because both depend on headings, labels and text alternatives. A volunteer coordinator page with real headings is more likely to be summarized correctly when someone asks an assistant where to volunteer in Palm Beach County.

One note on scope: this is a practical audit against WCAG 2.2 Level AA, the standard most organizations use as a target. It is not legal advice, and a quick audit does not replace a full conformance review of a large site.

Hour one: keyboard and focus

Unplug the mouse, or just stop touching it, and work through the pages that matter most: home, donate, volunteer, events and contact.

  • Press Tab from the top of each page. Can you reach every link, button and form field?
  • Can you always see where focus is? A visible focus ring is required, and many themes remove it.
  • Is there a skip link that jumps past the navigation to the main content?
  • Do menus, accordions and modals open with Enter or Space and close with Escape?
  • Can you finish the donation and volunteer forms end to end, including any payment step?

Write down every place you get stuck. Jason Colapietro starts here because keyboard failures usually point to template problems that repeat on every page. A donation form you cannot complete by keyboard is the highest-priority fix on most nonprofit sites.

Hour two: structure, headings and labels

Install a free checker such as WAVE or the axe DevTools extension, then look at what the page means to a machine:

  1. One h1 per page, then h2 and h3 in order with no skipped levels. Headings chosen for their size rather than their meaning are a common cause of confusion for screen reader users.
  2. Landmarks: header, nav, main and footer elements, so users can jump between regions.
  3. Form labels tied to every input. Placeholder text that disappears when you type is not a label.
  4. Error messages that say what went wrong in text, not just a red border.
  5. Link text that makes sense out of context. Ten links that all say "Learn more" are useless in a screen reader's link list.
  6. Page language set with lang, and set again on any section written in Spanish or Haitian Creole, which matters for many Palm Beach County audiences.

The same structural fixes appear in Suede's guide to five website fixes for West Palm Beach and Jupiter businesses, which explains why real words under real headings help both people and AI crawlers.

Hour three: images, color and media

  • Alt text on every meaningful image. Describe what the photo shows and why it is there: "Volunteers sorting produce at the Saturday distribution" rather than "IMG_4471". Decorative images get an empty alt="".
  • Text in images, such as event flyers posted as a single JPEG, needs the same information in real text on the page. This is the most common failure on community event pages.
  • Contrast of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Light gray body copy on white and white text on a pale brand color both fail often.
  • Color alone must never carry meaning, such as a red date for "sold out".
  • Video needs captions, and audio-only content such as a recorded board meeting needs a transcript.
  • Motion such as auto-advancing carousels should pause on request and respect the reduced-motion setting.

Hour four: mobile, zoom and documents

Open the site on a phone and zoom the desktop browser to 200 percent. Text should reflow without horizontal scrolling, and tap targets should be at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels under WCAG 2.2. Then check the PDFs. Annual reports, grant summaries and event packets are often scanned images with no readable text at all. If a PDF matters, publish the key content as an HTML page and keep the PDF as a download.

Finally, check that dates, hours and addresses are written in plain text sentences. That helps screen reader users and it helps AI assistants quote the right information, a point Suede covers in the guide to writing docs that AI assistants quote correctly.

Turning findings into a fix list

Sort what you found into three groups. Blockers stop someone from donating, volunteering or getting help, and get fixed this week. Serious issues make a task hard but possible. Everything else goes into the next site update. Most small nonprofits find that a handful of template-level fixes, such as restoring focus rings and labeling form fields, clear most of the list at once.

The Anti-Slop Templates Jason Colapietro published in this repository are built to that floor: semantic landmarks, a skip link, visible focus states, ordered headings and full support for reduced motion. They are a starting point, and every real build still needs its own review once content and forms are added.
