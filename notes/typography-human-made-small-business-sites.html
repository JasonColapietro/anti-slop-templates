Typography choices that read as human-made: a guide for small-business sites

The type decisions Suede AI makes on small-business builds in Palm Beach County: a pairing with a job, a fixed scale, the details people notice and fonts that survive the Florida sun.

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede AI · November 15, 2026 · West Palm Beach, Florida

Two bakeries in West Palm Beach can sell the same croissant and still look like different kinds of business online. One site sets every line in the same default sans serif at one weight, centered, with a gradient on the headline. The other uses a serif with a little warmth for headings, a plain text face for the menu and prices that line up in a column. Customers read the second one as a place run by people, and they are right to.

Typography is the fastest tell that a site was generated rather than designed. This guide covers the type decisions Suede AI makes on small-business builds, with examples from the Anti-Slop Templates in this repository and from two Suede concept sites.

Why default type reads as machine-made

Generic AI page builders converge on the same handful of choices because those choices are the statistical middle. You have seen the pattern: one geometric sans for everything, headings that differ from body text only in size, an all-caps eyebrow above every section, and centered paragraphs that run the full width of a laptop screen. None of it is wrong on its own. Together it says nobody chose anything.

A human-made type system shows decisions. It has a reason for its display face, a hierarchy you can feel before you read, and small details that only appear when someone cared about the text. Jason Colapietro built the Anti-Slop Templates around that idea: each of the two systems commits to a type pairing and writes it into one tokens file, so a business owner gets decisions instead of defaults.

Pick a pairing with a job description

Most small-business sites need two families and, sometimes, a monospace for numbers or code. Give each one a job:

  • Display face for headlines and pull quotes. This is where personality lives.
  • Text face for body copy, navigation, forms and menus. It should be plain, legible at 16px on a phone and available in the weights you need.
  • Optional mono for prices, hours, specifications or data tables.

The Editorial system pairs Fraunces with Hanken Grotesk and uses JetBrains Mono where tabular data appears. Fraunces has an optical size axis, so the same family looks sturdy at body size and more expressive at headline size. The Suede system pairs Instrument Serif with Manrope and the same mono, which gives it a colder, institutional voice. Neither pairing is a trend pick. Jason Colapietro matched each one to the brand voice it was written for.

The concept builds show two ends of the range. The Blueline Surf case study for a Jupiter surf and paddle shop uses Barlow Condensed for headings, which reads like signage on a board rack, with Inter for body text. The Palm & Peak Roofing concept for a Palm Beach County contractor uses Georgia headings over an Arial body, both system fonts, so the page downloads no font files at all. A roofer's customer is often on a phone during a storm warning, and that choice puts speed first.

Set a scale and stop adding sizes

Slop sites accumulate font sizes the way junk drawers accumulate batteries. Pick a scale once, name the steps in tokens and refuse to add new ones per page. Five or six steps cover nearly every small-business site: body, small, two or three heading levels and one display size for the hero.

Three numbers matter more than the rest:

  1. Body size of at least 16px, often 17 or 18px for a text face with small letterforms.
  2. Line length of roughly 60 to 75 characters for paragraphs. Use a max-width in ch units on prose so long lines never stretch across a wide monitor.
  3. Line height around 1.5 for body copy and tighter, near 1.1, for large headings.

Left-align body copy. Centered text works for a short hero line and fails for anything longer than two lines, because the eye has to hunt for the start of each row.

The small details that signal a person

These details take minutes and are almost never present in generated pages. Jason Colapietro checks for them before a Suede AI launch:

  • Curly quotes and real apostrophes instead of straight typewriter marks.
  • En dashes in ranges, such as hours of 7–3 or prices of $40–60.
  • Tabular figures on price lists with font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums, so the decimal points stack.
  • A non-breaking hyphen where a name must not wrap. The wordmark on this site uses &#8209; so "Anti-Slop" never splits.
  • Real italics from the family, not the browser's slanted fake, by loading the italic style you use.
  • Sentence-case headings. Title Case On Every Word reads like a template.

Design for South Florida glare and phone screens

Here is the local angle that changes real choices. Much of the small-business traffic in Palm Beach County arrives on phones, and a lot of it happens outdoors: someone checking a menu on Clematis Street at noon or looking up rental hours in a parking lot by the Jupiter Inlet. Thin weights and light gray text wash out in direct sun.

Jason Colapietro's rule for these Suede AI builds is to avoid weights below 400 for body text, keep text contrast at or above the WCAG AA ratio of 4.5:1 for normal text, and test the site on a real phone outside before launch. Hairline display weights can still work for a large headline, but never for prices, phone numbers or opening hours.

Load only what you use

Every weight and style is another file. A site that requests eight weights of two families pays for it in load time, and slow pages lose local customers before the first heading paints. Practical limits:

  • Two families plus an optional mono, with only the weights the design actually uses.
  • Variable fonts where available, since one file can cover a weight range.
  • font-display: swap and preconnect hints, as the pages in this repository use.
  • System fonts when speed matters more than voice, as on the roofing concept.

The tradeoff is honest: system fonts are fast and free but look the same as thousands of other sites, while a distinctive web font costs a few kilobytes and a licensing check. Google Fonts families like the ones used here are open-licensed, which suits most small businesses.
