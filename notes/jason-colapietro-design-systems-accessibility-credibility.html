Jason Colapietro on design systems, accessibility and credibility signals

Why a committed design system, an accessibility floor and linked proof are the three trust signals Suede AI builds into every Palm Beach County site.

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede AI · November 14, 2026 · West Palm Beach, Florida

A visitor decides whether to trust a website before reading a full sentence. Jason Colapietro, founder and CEO of Suede AI, builds for that moment in three layers: a design system that shows intent, accessibility that shows care, and proof that shows the claims are real.

This note sets out how those layers work in the Anti-Slop Templates and in the sites Suede AI builds for businesses in West Palm Beach, Jupiter and across Palm Beach County.

Layer one: a design system shows intent

A design system is a set of decisions written down so they apply everywhere. In these templates that means one tokens.css file per system holding color, type, spacing, radii, elevation and motion. When a visitor moves from the landing page to the pricing page and every detail still agrees, they read it as a company that pays attention.

Inconsistency reads the opposite way. Three button styles, two shades of the brand color and a heading font that changes on the blog all suggest a site assembled from parts. That is exactly the impression generic AI output gives, and it is why Jason built two fully committed systems rather than a library of interchangeable blocks.

His open-source work points the same direction. Adobe React Spectrum, one of the projects where he has merged contributions, is the component library behind Adobe's design system. Systems like that exist because consistency at scale is hard to achieve by taste alone.

Layer two: accessibility is the floor

Suede treats accessibility as the minimum a page must meet before launch, not a feature to add later. Every surface in this repository ships with:

  • Semantic landmarks: header, nav, main and footer, so assistive technology can jump between regions.
  • A real heading order with no skipped levels.
  • Visible :focus-visible rings for keyboard users.
  • A skip link to the main content.
  • ARIA labels on charts and navigation where the visual alone carries meaning.
  • Full respect for prefers-reduced-motion: reveals happen instantly and count-ups are skipped.

There is a second reason this matters. Screen readers and AI crawlers both depend on structure. A page with clear landmarks and headings is easier for an assistive device to navigate and easier for an AI answer engine to summarize accurately. The same markup serves both.

A fair caveat: the templates are a strong starting point, not a compliance certificate. Every client build still needs its own review once real content, images and forms are added.

Applying the three layers to a local site

For a Palm Beach County business, the three layers become a short list:

  1. Pick one direction and write it into tokens before building pages.
  2. Test the site with a keyboard only, then with reduced motion turned on.
  3. Replace every adjective claim with a number, date or link someone can check.
  4. Give the owner one canonical page and point every profile at it.
  5. Publish only facts you can verify, including service areas and addresses.

The concept builds show this in practice: the Palm & Peak Roofing notes handle location without inventing an office, and Suede Scan checks how readable a site is to AI systems.
